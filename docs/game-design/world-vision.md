# World vision

Status: settled design direction; named examples and the cause of the ancient decline remain open. See [resources and creation](resources-and-creation.md) for progression and the [roadmap](roadmap.md) for delivery.

## The player promise

The player is a dwarf in a dangerous, beautiful fantasy world whose older life was much larger. They can roam as a nomad, build a home, explore an unfamiliar environment, invent a machine, develop a spell or pursue a new combat possibility. Their ambitions determine their journey.

Wyram is a sandbox. The world offers places, materials, inhabitants and dependable interactions that inspire player projects. A mandatory story, quest sequence or prescribed completion route must not become the organizing structure of progression. The long-term creative ambition is the player's imagination; each implemented system should offer room for useful combinations beyond its tutorial examples.

## Populations of mythic colossi

Colossi were populations of mythic beasts with recognizable lineages. Some forms are familiar enough to evoke dragons, leviathans or horned giants; others have much stranger anatomy. A lineage can include related species and individuals of different ages and sizes. Exact taxonomy and species counts remain content decisions.

Repeated anatomical features make relationships discoverable: horns, joints, teeth, branching ribs, plates or wing structures. Herd remains, nesting grounds, migration traces and fossil concentrations suggest populations and former ecosystems. A named individual can acquire a local legend while still belonging to a population.

Their massive remains serve several purposes together:

- Landmarks that change how a player reads and navigates a landscape.
- Habitats and shelter for present life, including dwarven settlements.
- Physical evidence of the previous world and its changing scale.
- The most powerful natural resources, with different properties across lineages.

Bone material has gameplay value. A skeleton used as architecture must also participate in the resource rules. Extraction can transform the landmark and the lives around it. Settlement boundaries or cultural restrictions, when introduced, need visible reasons and consistent interactions.

## A world that feels as though it is shrinking

The dwarf is small, ancient creatures are massive, and the environment spans the space between them. Average and colossal mountains, vegetation and other features coexist. Preserve large living vegetation and monumental terrain alongside smaller surroundings; uniform enlargement would erase the contrast.

Older remnants often suggest a greater scale than the life now surrounding them. This recurring pattern silently hints at why the colossi died. The player encounters evidence before explanations.

Possible environmental clues include ordinary woodland growing around ancient giant trunks, repeated skeletons near a vanished water source, small reef life occupying leviathan bones, or a frozen beast preserving features absent from weathered skeletons. These are authoring examples. Literal ongoing contraction, the mechanism of decline, and a universal rule that every younger individual is smaller are not settled canon.

Compose scenes at three scales:

| Scale | Role in the experience | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dwarf reach | Interaction, comfort and an immediate sense of measure | Shards, tools, roots, doorways, workshops, places to shelter. |
| Inhabited surroundings | Traversal, building and ordinary local life | Trees, houses, cliffs, live animals, sections of a skeleton. |
| Ancient monument | Awe, orientation and invitations to travel | Whole colossi, immense canopies, mountain walls, distant remains. |

A small workshop inside a rib makes the rib's scale legible. Large spaces need routes, detail and places to occupy. Buildability and readable movement remain part of the scene, even when its silhouette is extraordinary.

The extinction's cause remains unresolved in the early game. Cultures can offer contradictory interpretations grounded in their circumstances. The shrinking-world motif supports the mystery without requiring exposition or a canonical answer.

## Dwarven societies

The world's intelligent peoples are different dwarven lineages or races. They live as nomads, including players, or in settlements with their own identities. Separate lineage, culture and expertise: ancestry does not determine friendliness, morality or technical achievement.

A settlement's identity can draw on its environment, available resources, dwarven lineage, values, expertise and relationship to nearby colossi. Its inhabitants need plausible reasons to live there and recognizable ways of using that place.

The original settlement directions are:

- Skilled smiths living inside colossus bones in an open desert.
- Dark-magic practitioners living in the abyssal ocean.
- Forest hunters and gatherers pursuing a simpler way of life.

Other combinations remain possible. Communities with simpler tools can have sophisticated craft traditions, particularly around easily worked bone shards. Their creations deserve identities of their own.

For the initial settlement scope, cities coexist without intercity war, attacks, diplomacy or geopolitical simulation. Local inhabitants may welcome, watch, warn or attack a player who approaches or crosses a boundary. Those behaviors arrive through bounded gameplay slices. Settlements should feel inhabited even when they supply no quests.

Names such as Ribforges, Nhal-Azur or Pale Archive from the earlier brainstorm are working examples, with no fixed roster implied.

## Four connected frontiers

The surface is the main environment. Underground, ocean and skies must also give players reasons to explore and, eventually, to live there. Their appeal comes from distinctive possibilities, atmosphere and ways of inhabiting them.

| Frontier | Range of environments | Reasons to explore | Reasons to make a home |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Surface | Forests, plains, deserts, mountain slopes and peaks, frozen or volcanic country | Broad material variety, settlements, visible colossi, accessible routes into other environments. | Many building settings, nearby resources, social contact and easy connections to other frontiers. |
| Underground | Shallow caves, mines, great caverns, fossil beds, underground forests and water bodies | Exposed anatomy, minerals, sheltered ecosystems, ancient traces and specialized creation materials. | Cavern architecture, workshop conditions, protected spaces and local resources. |
| Ocean | Coasts, reefs, open water, twilight depths and abyss | Aquatic lineages, unusual resources, submerged remains and cultures adapted to depth. | Coastal or submerged architecture, distinct production possibilities and an ocean way of life. |
| Skies | High peaks, floating islands, cloud environments and storm regions | Aerial lineages, suspended remains, altitude-specific life and new navigation possibilities. | Elevated architecture, aerial routes and local materials or conditions supporting distinctive projects. |

The listed environments are design space, not a promise that they all ship in the first expansion. Dangers and practical habitation requirements must be authored alongside their rewards.

Frontiers overlap geographically and mechanically. Mountain peaks bridge surface and skies. Caverns and underground water bodies can connect to the ocean. A coastline, cliff, flooded passage or exposed skeleton can belong to more than one frontier. These connections should be traversable where the released capabilities support them; future routes may be foreshadowed visibly.

A frontier must support more than a short visit for a rare ingredient. Mature releases should make local building, resource use, continued exploration and everyday life worthwhile. A dwelling there can remain connected to the broader world while supporting its own projects.

## Atmosphere and readable danger

Aim for awe, curiosity, vulnerability and the desire to inhabit a place. Danger gains meaning when the player can notice it, prepare for it and decide what to attempt. Beauty and comfort give them something to protect or return to.

Use silhouettes, material textures, sound, light, vegetation, inhabitants and environmental traces together. A lineage's bone should be recognizable in both a distant skeleton and a dwarven creation. A settlement's crafts should reveal its surroundings and choices.

Simple initial biomes may establish a frontier's landscape before its full ecology or societies arrive. Do not represent that landscape as a completed frontier. Later releases add its life, practices, materials, threats and opportunities while revisiting places already introduced.
